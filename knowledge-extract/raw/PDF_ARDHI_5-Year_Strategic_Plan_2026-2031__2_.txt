1. Executive Summary 
ARDHI is a Ugandan based Non-Government Organisation driving community resilience through advocacy and 
systems innovations. We started community works in 2024 and were fully registered in 2025. This 5-Year 
Strategic Plan (2026–2031) sets out how ARDHI will grow from a lean, credible start-up NGO into a recognized 
systems-change institution across six thematic pillars: legal and policy influence, agriculture and food security, 
waste management, disaster preparedness, public health, and inclusive land governance. 
The plan is deliberately built for a young organization. It prioritizes credibility, adaptability, and phased growth 
over heavy infrastructure, while still setting measurable targets, a realistic financial trajectory, and clear 
governance and risk safeguards that a Board, partners, and donors can hold ARDHI accountable to. 
Over five years, ARDHI's annual operating budget is projected to grow from approximately UGX 380 million in 
Year 1 to approximately UGX 2.3 billion in Year 5, supported by a diversified funding mix of institutional grants, 
consultancies, research commissions, partnerships, and innovation grants. By the end of Year 5, ARDHI aims to 
have directly reached over 150,000 community members, influenced at least 8 policies or regulatory frameworks, 
and institutionalized at least 10 proven innovation models. 
This Strategic Plan is complemented by a companion ARDHI Sustainability and Continuity Plan (2026–2031), 
which sets out in full ARDHI's global and national alignment (SDGs and Uganda's NDP IV), financial reserve policy, 
institutional memory and succession systems, partnership governance, and risk-continuity protocols.

2. About ARDHI 
ARDHI exists as a high-impact advocacy, research, and innovation organization that strengthens communities 
through law, agriculture and food security, waste management, disaster preparedness, public health, and 
inclusive land governance. Established in 2024 and fully registered in 2025, ARDHI is a relatively young 
organization — lean, mobile, and project-driven — operating without a permanent office building or a large 
permanent staff complement. 
This plan assumes a start-up NGO environment: shared office space, mobile operations, lean staffing, volunteer 
and consultant support, project-based implementation, and gradual institutional growth. It also assumes a 
dynamic operating environment shaped by funding competition, climate risk, urban service pressures, policy 
uncertainty, and rising expectations for measurable impact. 
2.1 Strategic Position 
ARDHI's competitive advantage lies in its integrated model: advocacy shifts systems, research generates 
evidence, and innovation tests practical models that communities and institutions can use. This model is 
especially powerful in Uganda, where food security, land governance, public health, waste management, and 
disaster readiness are deeply interconnected. This strategic plan treats ARDHI as a systems-change organization 
rather than a project-delivery NGO. 
2.2 Vision, Mission and Values 
Vision: A future where research informs action, and action delivers justice. 
Mission: Impacting communities through research, innovations and advocacy — particularly in smart agriculture, 
waste management, disaster preparedness, health, and land governance. 
Values 
• Sustainability — Champion policies and actions that safeguard natural resources. 
• Equity — Foster inclusive environments and advocate for fair access to resources, ensuring marginalized 
voices are represented and empowered. 
• Innovation — Pioneer creative solutions and leverage emerging technologies to address complex systemic 
challenges. 
• Collaboration — Forge purposeful partnerships to amplify collective impact and drive lasting change.

3. Theory of Change 
ARDHI is not a Think Tank, but a Think and Act Tank. We operate on the belief that if we combine advocacy to 
shift rules, research to generate empirical evidence, and innovation to pilot practical models — across 
agriculture, waste, disaster preparedness, health, and land governance — communities can gain the tools, voice, 
and systems they need to solve persistent problems. Lasting impact requires more than ideas: it requires 
pressure, proof, and prototypes working together.

6. Strategic Pillars 
Pillar 1: Advocacy 
Advocacy is the mechanism through which ARDHI influences laws, policies, institutions, and public behaviour. In 
law, advocacy focuses on rights, fairness, accountability, and land governance. In agriculture, it pushes for food 
security, inclusive support, and climate-resilient policy. In waste, disaster preparedness, and public health, 
advocacy drives safer systems and stronger public responsibility. 
ARDHI's advocacy model is evidence-led, coalition-based, and solution-oriented. It includes policy briefs, public 
forums, legal commentary, community voices, media engagement, and institutional dialogue. It remains non-
partisan while still being firm, strategic, and transformative. 
Pillar 2: Research 
Research is ARDHI's evidence engine. It produces baseline studies, policy analysis, legal reviews, community 
diagnostics, needs assessments, case documentation, and learning products that inform advocacy and 
innovation. Research is not treated as an academic exercise alone; it is the practical foundation for action. 
ARDHI's research agenda focuses on the real conditions behind community vulnerability: land conflict, 
agricultural stress, waste risk, health inequity, disaster exposure, and weak implementation systems. Over five 
years, the organization becomes a recognized source of credible, policy-relevant evidence in its thematic areas. 
Pillar 3: Innovation 
Innovation is ARDHI's translation layer between ideas and usable practice. It includes pilots, prototypes, service 
models, community tools, digital approaches, local campaigns, and demonstration projects. Innovation is 
especially important for a young NGO because it allows impact without waiting for large infrastructure or 
permanent offices. 
ARDHI's innovations are practical, low-cost, replicable, and partnership-friendly: demo plots or advisory tools in 
agriculture; segregation pilots or community collection models in waste; risk maps, preparedness checklists, and 
response simulations in disaster preparedness; simplified guides, rights tools, and dispute-resolution support 
systems in law and land governance.

7. Thematic Strategy 
ArdhiAgriculture 
Focuses on food security, resilient livelihoods, and climate-smart practice — farmer learning, production 
resilience, value-chain awareness, and policy support that improves access to inputs, knowledge, and markets. 
ArdhiWaste 
Focuses on safe, responsible, circular approaches to waste — documenting risks, promoting policy accountability, 
and piloting practical models for segregation, recovery, and local value creation. Treated as a health and urban 
governance issue, not only an environmental one. 
ArdhiDisasters 
Focuses on anticipation, readiness, and rapid response — mapping risks, strengthening community preparedness, 
supporting local planning, and advocating for responsive institutions to help communities reduce losses before 
shocks become disasters. 
ArdhiHealth 
Focuses on the intersection of environment, settlement conditions, land, waste, and access to services — treating 
public health as a cross-cutting outcome of better governance, cleaner environments, safer communities, and 
more informed institutions. 
ArdhiLandGovernance 
Focuses on fairness, access, tenure awareness, dispute reduction, and inclusive decision-making — supporting 
community rights, policy reform, and practical tools that help ordinary people navigate land systems more 
effectively. 
ArdhiLaw 
Supports legal empowerment, policy reform, rights protection, and institutional accountability — connecting 
legal analysis with community realities and strengthening every other thematic area by providing a formal 
pathway for reform and redress.

9. Strategic Objectives by Year 
Year 1: Consolidation of Foundations 
Institutional stabilization: governance, finance, administration, communications, research management and 
program delivery systems established. Branding, partnership mapping, baseline studies, and a small but capable 
core team finalized. Priority is strong internal order, not large-scale expansion. ARDHI operates from shared or 
rented workspace with a lean staffing model, proving it can function credibly without a permanent building. 
Year 2: Pilot Expansion and Visibility 
Pilot activities expand in each thematic area. ARDHI launches a small number of high-quality demonstration 
initiatives that show clear community value and generate usable evidence. Communications, media visibility and 
stakeholder confidence strengthen. Fundraising, donor diversification and partnership credibility grow as ARDHI 
moves from start-up status toward recognized specialist status. 
Year 3: Scaling What Works 
Proven interventions are scaled — adapted for more communities, districts, or institutional partners. Pilots 
convert into repeatable packages, manuals, and partner-led implementation formats. Research and advocacy 
outputs gain stronger traction in public systems and ARDHI's thematic brand becomes more defined. 
Year 4: Institutional Influence 
ARDHI positions as a trusted thought leader and convening actor: hosting dialogues, publishing stronger policy 
products, supporting multi-stakeholder coalitions, and expanding public and private partnerships. Operationally, 
ARDHI becomes less project-dependent and more system-oriented, with stronger reserves, clearer talent 
systems, and a more mature monitoring framework. 
Year 5: Sustainability, Replication and Renewal 
ARDHI consolidates gains and prepares the next strategy cycle — documenting what works, retiring what does 
not, institutionalizing successful models, and preparing a refreshed five-year plan based on actual learning. Focus 
is on continuity, scale readiness, and long-term legitimacy.

10. Financial Model 
ARDHI's financial sustainability depends on a diversified portfolio of funding sources rather than a single donor 
stream: institutional grants, consultancies, training services, research commissions, convening support, 
innovation grants, and strategic partnerships. Unrestricted income is prioritized because it protects the core 
functions that keep the institution alive. 
The financial target is gradual stabilization: enough core funding to sustain staff, compliance, and knowledge 
systems, plus flexible funds for innovation. Reserves, monthly cash-flow discipline, and strong grant management 
are non-negotiable. All figures below are projections in Ugandan Shillings (UGX) and will be reviewed annually 
against actual funds secured. 
10.1 Reserve Policy 
ARDHI maintains a minimum reserve policy equivalent to at least three months of essential operating costs, built 
deliberately into the annual budget (Reserve Building, Section 10.2) rather than left to year-end surplus. Full 
reserve-policy governance, cash-flow discipline, and financial continuity arrangements are set out in the 
companion ARDHI Sustainability and Continuity Plan.

11. Resource Mobilization Strategy 
ARDHI will pursue funding across five parallel tracks, each with a lead directorate and annual targets reviewed by 
the Board's Finance & Audit Committee: 
• Institutional grants — Multi-year grants from bilateral, multilateral and foundation donors active in land 
governance, climate resilience, and public health in East Africa. Target: 2–3 active grants by Year 2, 5+ by 
Year 5. 
• Consultancies & training — Fee-based technical assistance, training delivery, and facilitation for government 
and private-sector partners, building on the AIMS AI grant-writing assistant to lower proposal turnaround 
time. 
• Research commissions — Commissioned studies, baseline surveys and policy reviews for donors, 
government agencies and coalition partners. 
• Partnerships & coalitions — Cost-shared and in-kind collaboration with peer NGOs, universities, and private-
sector actors, reducing per-partner delivery cost. 
• Innovation & challenge grants — Competitive grants and hackathon-linked funding (e.g., Africa's Talking, AI 
Studio Uganda networks) for piloting digital and low-cost tools. 
A rolling 18-month fundraising pipeline will be maintained by the Partnerships & Mobilisation directorate, 
reviewed monthly against the targets in Section 10. ARDHI's broader partnership system — including MOU 
standards, co-funding arrangements, and shared learning — is set out in the companion ARDHI Sustainability and 
Continuity Plan.

13. Safeguarding, Gender and Inclusion 
Given ARDHI's work with vulnerable communities in land, disaster, and health contexts, safeguarding and 
inclusion are treated as non-negotiable operating commitments, not optional add-ons. 
• Safeguarding — All staff, consultants and volunteers sign and are trained on a Safeguarding Policy covering 
protection from sexual exploitation and abuse (PSEA), child protection, and a confidential reporting and 
referral pathway. 
• Gender — Program design applies a gender lens across all six pillars (e.g., women's land rights under 
ArdhiLandGovernance, gender-responsive disaster planning under ArdhiDisasters); gender-disaggregated 
data collection is mandatory in the M&E framework. 
• Disability & inclusion — Community engagement formats (forums, USSD/voice tools, printed materials) are 
designed to be accessible to persons with disabilities and low-literacy participants. 
• Environmental & social risk screening — Innovation pilots are screened for environmental and social risk 
before rollout, consistent with donor safeguard requirements.

14. Communications and Visibility Strategy 
Communications is treated as a strategic function that builds ARDHI's credibility, donor confidence, and advocacy 
reach — not a support afterthought. 
• Brand consistency — All public materials use ARDHI's brand identity (navy, mint, forest green, orange-red) 
and the Advocacy | Research | Innovation tagline. 
• Digital-first visibility — Active presence across social media, a maintained website, and thematic content 
aligned to the six ArdhiX sub-brands. 
• Policy communications — Policy briefs and research findings translated into accessible formats 
(infographics, short video, radio) for non-technical audiences. 
• Media relations — A maintained press/media contact list and rapid-response protocol for commenting on 
breaking policy or disaster events within ARDHI's mandate. 
• Donor and partner visibility — Quarterly impact updates and an annual report shared with all active donors 
and partners.

15. Monitoring, Evaluation and Learning 
ARDHI measures progress through outcome indicators, not just activity counts. Key measures include policy 
influence achieved, communities reached, models piloted, models scaled, partnerships activated, funds 
diversified, and knowledge products produced (see Section 9.1 for the full Results Framework). The organization 
also tracks institutional health indicators such as staff retention, reserve cover, and program continuity — set out 
in full as sustainability indicators in the companion ARDHI Sustainability and Continuity Plan. 
15.1 Learning Cycle 
• Quarterly reflection — Directorate-level review of progress against KPIs, feeding into Board reporting. 
• Annual review — Full results-framework review against Section 9.1 targets, published as an annual report. 
• Strategy refresh — Light-touch review at the Year 3 midpoint; full strategy renewal at the start of Year 5 
(Section 16). 
This learning cycle allows ARDHI to adapt quickly without losing direction — keeping the organization innovative 
and grounded at the same time.

16. Five-Year End State 
At the end of five years, ARDHI is a stronger, more visible, and more influential organization with a clear niche in 
advocacy, research, and innovation. It operates with a lean but stable structure, a credible funding mix (Section 
10.4), demonstrable community impact (Section 9.1), and a growing network of trusted partners. Its programs 
are no longer experimental only; they are becoming repeatable, documented, and institutionally useful. 
ARDHI's final position is that of a mature young NGO: still flexible, still innovative, but now strategically grounded, 
operationally disciplined, and ready for deeper scale and policy influence. 
This Strategic Plan is complemented by the ARDHI Sustainability and Continuity Plan (2026–2031), which sets out 
in full ARDHI's financial reserve policy, institutional memory and succession systems, partnership governance, 
and organizational risk-continuity protocols.
