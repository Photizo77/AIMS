About ARDHI
We are a Ugandan based NGO that drives community resilience through advocacy and systems innovations in the areas of agriculture and food security, waste management, disaster preparedness, and land governance.
Vision
A future where research informs action, and action delivers justice.
Mission
Impacting communities through research, innovations and advocacy particularly in areas of smart agriculture, waste management, disaster preparedness, health and land governance.
Our Values
Sustainability: Champion policies and actions that safeguard natural resources.
Equity: Foster inclusive environments and advocate for fair access to resources, ensuring marginalized voices are represented and empowered.
Innovation: Pioneer creative solutions and leverage emerging technologies to address complex systemic challenges.
Collaboration: Forge purposeful partnerships to amplify collective impact and drive lasting change.
Our Theory of Change
ARDHI is not a Think Tank, but a Think and Act Tank. Established in 2024 and fully registered in 2025, we operate on the belief that if we combine advocacy to shift rules, research to generate empirical evidence, and innovation to pilot practical models across agriculture, waste, disaster preparedness, health, and land governance, communities can gain the tools, voice, and systems they need to solve persistent problems. Lasting impact requires more than just ideas, it requires pressure, proof, and prototypes working together.
Our offices are located at Plot 1207, Ggaba Road, Next to the American Embassy, Kampala, Uganda. For general enquiries, contact us at info@ardhi.org.ug or visit www.ardhi.org.ug.

1. Purpose
This annex sets out how ARDHI Law and Policy Initiative decides who benefits from its projects, and the tools used to apply that decision fairly and openly. Beneficiary selection is one of the most sensitive parts of any project. Where selection is unclear, benefits flow to those who are already connected, and the people the project was designed for are left out. Where selection is clear, published and verified, the project builds trust rather than resentment.
If a person cannot find out why their neighbour was selected and they were not, the selection process has failed, however good the outcome looks on paper.
1.1 Objectives
To reach the people who are most affected by insecure land rights and least able to secure them on their own.
To make the criteria public, understandable and applied in the same way everywhere.
To reduce the risk of elite capture, favouritism and exclusion.
To give any person who is not selected a way to ask why and to appeal.
To produce a verified beneficiary record that can withstand a donor audit.
1.2 Principles of targeting

Principle
 | What it means in practice
 | 
Need first
 | Selection is driven by vulnerability and by the severity of the land rights problem, not by connections, ability to pay or ease of access.
 | 
Transparency
 | Criteria, scores, timelines and lists are displayed publicly and explained in the local language before selection starts.
 | 
Participation
 | The community, including women, youth and persons with disabilities, takes part in identifying and validating beneficiaries.
 | 
Inclusion
 | Quotas protect groups that would otherwise be missed. Reasonable accommodation removes barriers to applying.
 | 
Consistency
 | The same criteria and the same scoring are used across all locations, so that a person in one village is treated as a person in another.
 | 
Verification
 | No list is final until it has been checked independently against the criteria and against reality on the ground.
 | 
Do no harm
 | Selection does not expose anyone to conflict, stigma or retaliation. Sensitive categories are never announced publicly.
 | 
Appealable
 | Every decision can be questioned and reviewed within a stated time.
 | 
Documented
 | Every step is recorded so that the process can be reconstructed years later.
 | 

2. Categories of Beneficiary

Category
 | Definition
 | Typical support received
 | 
Direct individual beneficiaries
 | Named individuals or households who receive a service such as legal aid, mediation, documentation support or training.
 | Case handling, documentation, training, accompaniment
 | 
Direct group beneficiaries
 | Community structures such as communal land associations, women land rights groups, area land committees, youth groups.
 | Capacity building, facilitation, small grants, equipment
 | 
Institutional beneficiaries
 | Local government departments, land offices, local council courts, clan structures.
 | Training, tools, systems support, joint activities
 | 
Indirect beneficiaries
 | Household members and community members who benefit because a direct beneficiary was supported, or who are reached through radio and public information.
 | Information, improved services, reduced conflict
 | 
Indirect beneficiaries are estimated using a documented and conservative method, usually household size from the most recent national census applied to direct beneficiary households, plus verified radio reach. ARDHI never inflates reach figures and always states the method used.

Districts, sub counties and parishes are selected before individuals, using the following criteria. Each is scored from 1 to 5 and weighted.

 | 25 percent

Level of tenure insecurity, measured by the proportion of land without any document
 | 20 percent
 | District land office data, national survey data, rapid assessment
 | 
Poverty level and human development indicators
 | 15 percent
 | Uganda Bureau of Statistics data, district development plan
 | 
Presence of groups at high risk of exclusion, including women headed households, refugees, pastoralists and informal settlement residents
 | 15 percent
 | Census data, district community development office
 |

 | 
Willingness of the district and sub county to cooperate, evidenced in writing
 | 10 percent
 | Letters of support, memoranda of understanding
 | 
Practical feasibility: access, security and cost of reaching the area
 | 5 percent

 | 3.3 Inclusion quotas
Regardless of ranking, the final selected list for any project must meet the following minimum composition. Where a quota is not met, the selection committee returns to the community to identify further eligible applicants rather than filling the place from the general list.

Group
 | Minimum share of direct beneficiaries
 | 
Women
 | 50 percent
 | 
Persons with disabilities
 | 10 percent, or the local prevalence rate if higher
 | 
Young people aged 18 to 30
 | 25 percent
 | 
Older persons aged 60 and above
 | 10 percent
 | 
Households with no land document of any kind
 | 60 percent
 | 
Refugee or displaced households, in hosting districts
 | Proportionate to their share of the sub county population
 | 3.4 Exclusion criteria
The following are not eligible, and the reason is explained openly at community meetings so that the rule is understood rather than resented:
Serving staff, board members, volunteers and consultants of ARDHI and of implementing partners, and members of their immediate households.
Persons who provided false information in order to be selected.
Applicants whose claim to the land is contested by a person with a stronger legitimate right, until that matter is resolved.
Persons seeking support to advance a claim against a lawful or bona fide occupant, or to complete a transaction that lacks required spousal consent.
Persons already receiving the same service for the same matter from another provider.
Applicants who refuse to consent to the collection and verification of the information required.
Where a staff member has a family connection to an applicant, the connection is declared in writing and that staff member takes no part in the assessment of that applicant. All declared conflicts are recorded in the conflict of interest register.

 | 4.1 Selection Committee
The Selection Committee is constituted for each sub county and has at least seven members, of whom at least half are women:
The ARDHI Project Manager or Field Officer, as chair.
One representative of the sub county administration or community development office.
Two representatives of the community, elected at a public meeting, one woman and one man.
One representative of a women land rights group.
One representative of an organisation of persons with disabilities, or a person with a disability elected by the community.
One youth representative.
One representative of the implementing partner where there is one.
Committee members sign a declaration of impartiality and the ARDHI Code of Conduct, and declare any interest in any applicant. Decisions are taken by consensus, and where consensus is not possible by a recorded vote. All decisions and reasons are minuted.
4.2 Appeals

Stage
 | Who decides
 | Timeline
 | 
Appeal lodged, in writing or orally to any ARDHI officer or through the grievance box
 | Received by the Field Officer
 | Within 14 days of publication of the list
 | 
First review against the criteria and the scoring sheet
 | Appeals Panel: Project Manager, MEL Officer and one community representative who was not on the Selection Committee
 | Within 15 working days of receipt
 | 
Final review
 | Country Director
 | Within 10 working days of the first decision
 | 
Communication of the decision, with reasons, in a language the appellant understands
 | Field Officer
 | Within 5 working days of the decision
 | An appeal is free. No person loses any existing entitlement because they appealed. Where an appeal succeeds, the person is added to the list and, where the list is full, is placed at the top of the waiting list for the next cycle.

5. Verification, Data and Safeguards
5.1 Verification standards
At least 10 percent of selected beneficiaries are verified by an independent home visit before services begin.
At least 5 percent of rejected applicants are verified, to confirm that rejection was correctly applied. This check matters as much as the first.
Verification is carried out by a person who took no part in the original scoring.
Where verification finds an error rate above 5 percent, the whole list for that location is rescreened.
Verification findings are reported to the Country Director and are recorded in the MEL system.
5.2 Data protection and confidentiality
Only the data needed to apply the criteria is collected.
Published lists show names and villages only. Scores, disability status, health information and details of the land problem are never displayed publicly.
Each beneficiary receives a unique code, which is used in all analysis files.
Consent is obtained at registration and covers the collection, storage, use and retention of the data.
Personal data is handled in accordance with the Data Protection and Privacy Act, 2019, and is destroyed at the end of the retention period.
5.3 Anti capture safeguards

Risk
 | Safeguard
 | 
Leaders direct selection to relatives and allies
 | Criteria published in advance; community validation meeting; independent verification; mixed selection committee; appeal route
 | 
Payment or favours demanded in exchange for selection
 | Written and repeated statement that all services are free; complaints box at every meeting; two officer rule; immediate handling under Annex 11
 | 
Ghost or duplicate beneficiaries
 | Unique codes; identity verification; deduplication across projects; sample home visits; attendance signatures matched to the register
 | 
Quiet exclusion of the least visible people
 | Mobile registration for those who cannot travel; door to door identification in the poorest zones; quotas that must be met before a list is approved
 | 
Women applying but being replaced by male household heads
 | Registration in the name of the actual rights holder; separate consultation with women; verification of who received the service
 | 
Selection creating conflict in the community
 | Explain the reason for every criterion at a public meeting; publish the number of places available from the outset; maintain a transparent waiting list
 | 6. Exit, Replacement and Waiting List
A beneficiary exits when the agreed service is complete, when they withdraw, when they move away, or when the information they gave is found to be false.
Every exit is recorded with the reason, and the beneficiary is informed in writing or in person.
A place that becomes free is filled from the ranked waiting list for the same village, applying the same quotas.
The waiting list is displayed publicly alongside the selected list, so that people can see where they stand.
Where a person is removed for giving false information, they may appeal, and the removal is recorded.
