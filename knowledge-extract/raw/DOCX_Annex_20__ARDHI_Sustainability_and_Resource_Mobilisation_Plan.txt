About ARDHI
We are a Ugandan based NGO that drives community resilience through advocacy and systems innovations in the areas of agriculture and food security, waste management, disaster preparedness, and land governance.
Vision
A future where research informs action, and action delivers justice.
Mission
Impacting communities through research, innovations and advocacy particularly in areas of smart agriculture, waste management, disaster preparedness, health and land governance.
Our Values
Sustainability: Champion policies and actions that safeguard natural resources.
Equity: Foster inclusive environments and advocate for fair access to resources, ensuring marginalized voices are represented and empowered.
Innovation: Pioneer creative solutions and leverage emerging technologies to address complex systemic challenges.
Collaboration: Forge purposeful partnerships to amplify collective impact and drive lasting change.
Our Theory of Change
ARDHI is not a Think Tank, but a Think and Act Tank. Established in 2024 and fully registered in 2025, we operate on the belief that if we combine advocacy to shift rules, research to generate empirical evidence, and innovation to pilot practical models across agriculture, waste, disaster preparedness, health, and land governance, communities can gain the tools, voice, and systems they need to solve persistent problems. Lasting impact requires more than just ideas, it requires pressure, proof, and prototypes working together.
Our offices are located at Plot 1207, Ggaba Road, Next to the American Embassy, Kampala, Uganda. For general enquiries, contact us at info@ardhi.org.ug or visit www.ardhi.org.ug.
1. Purpose and Context
This annex sets out how the results of ARDHI work continue after a project ends, and how ARDHI Law and Policy Initiative will fund itself over the coming years. The two questions belong together. An organisation that cannot sustain itself cannot sustain its results, and results that depend entirely on the presence of one organisation were never sustainable in the first place.
ARDHI is positioned as a research and innovation organisation in a sector where most actors do advocacy alone. That position is the foundation of its sustainability strategy, because it opens income sources that pure advocacy organisations cannot reach, including research contracts, technical services, training, and partnerships with universities and government.
Our aim is not to be present in a community forever. It is to leave behind people, structures and rules that no longer need us.
1.1 Objectives
To define what sustainability means for ARDHI across five dimensions and to plan for each one.
To set out a clear exit and transition approach for every project from the day it is designed.
To diversify ARDHI income so that no single funder holds a controlling share.
To build unrestricted reserves and cost recovery so that core functions are not dependent on project grants.
To develop earned income that fits the mission rather than distracting from it.
To set targets, responsibilities and a monitoring rhythm for resource mobilisation.

2. Sustainability Framework
ARDHI plans sustainability across five dimensions. A project is not considered well designed unless it addresses all five.

Dimension
 | Question it answers
 | How ARDHI addresses it
 | 
Institutional
 | Will the structures created keep working?
 | Work through existing institutions wherever possible: area land committees, local council courts, clan structures, communal land associations, district land offices. Where a new structure is needed, register it, give it a constitution, and link it to a permanent local institution before the project closes.
 | 
Human and technical
 | Will the skills stay in the community?
 | Train local people rather than deploying outsiders. Certify community paralegals and mediators. Train the trainers. Leave written and pictorial materials in the local language. Hold refresher clinics beyond the project period.
 | 
Financial
 | Will the activity be affordable without the project?
 | Design low cost models. Build modest local revenue for community structures, such as membership contributions and small service fees agreed by the community. Link structures to district budgets and to other funders.
 | 
Policy and legal
 | Will the change be written into rules?
 | Convert practice into policy: district ordinances and byelaws, written clan resolutions, land office procedures, and national policy submissions. A rule outlives a project.
 | 
Social and cultural
 | Will people keep doing it?
 | Work with clan leaders and men as allies so that changes in inheritance and spousal consent practice are owned locally. Use community dialogue rather than instruction. Celebrate and publicise local champions.
 | 2.1 Sustainability by design
Every project proposal includes a sustainability section that answers all five dimensions above and is reviewed before submission.
Every logframe includes at least one indicator that measures whether something will continue, for example the number of structures still functioning six months after support ends.
Every project budget includes a transition line in the final six months for handover, documentation and follow up.
Cost per beneficiary is calculated for every model, so that ARDHI can say honestly what continuation would cost and who could pay it.
Where an approach cannot be sustained, ARDHI says so at design and treats it as a time limited intervention rather than pretending otherwise.

Exit is planned from the start and is discussed openly with communities at entry, so that no one is surprised. ARDHI never leaves without a written handover.

4. Resource Mobilisation Strategy
4.1 Current position and the problem to solve
Like most Ugandan civil society organisations working on land, ARDHI depends heavily on a small number of restricted project grants. This creates three problems: core functions such as finance, governance and MEL are underfunded because donors prefer to fund activities; the end of one grant creates an immediate staffing crisis; and the organisation has little freedom to respond to an urgent land rights issue that no grant covers.
The strategy below is designed to solve those three problems in that order: fund the core, spread the risk, and create money that ARDHI itself controls.

6. Organisational Capacity for Resource Mobilisation
6.1 The grant writer scheme
ARDHI has established a grant writer scheme that draws writers from across its departments, so that fundraising capacity is spread through the organisation rather than concentrated in one person. Its main features are:
Writers are onboarded by Human Resources like any other member of staff, with a contract that covers how they write, manage and deliver grants.
Each writer has a weekly target set by the person allocating the calls, sized according to the complexity of each grant.
A weekly meeting, physical or online, reviews progress against targets.
ARDHI sources and supplies calls to writers, and indicates for each call whether it is a self submission or an organisation submission.
All proposals go onto the internal system before submission, are reviewed by the grant review team, and are revised until the review team is satisfied.
ARDHI supports writers with budget development, activity design, digital writing aids and artificial intelligence credits.
An agreed percentage is paid to the writer once a grant is won.
Writers have first consideration to join the implementation team of a grant they wrote, where it falls in their area, and are also considered for implementation of grants written by others, so that the benefit is spread across the team.
Writers may be asked to write articles related to grants and to tag the organisation, and ARDHI supports publication.
The terms are set so that they never place the organisation in a financial trap.

7. Earned Income Development
Earned income gives ARDHI money it controls. It must be built carefully so that it strengthens rather than distracts from the mission. Every earned income line below draws directly on capability ARDHI already needs for its programme work.

Service
 | Client
 | Why ARDHI can offer it credibly
 | Development steps
 | 
Land tenure and land governance research
 | Government, development partners, universities, private sector
 | Existing research capability and field networks; the research and innovation positioning
 | Publish a service profile; register on procurement portals; assemble a consultant roster; build a rate card
 | 
Evaluations and assessments
 | NGOs, donors, government programmes
 | MEL framework and tools already in place (Annex 14)
 | Build an evaluator panel; complete two reference assignments; obtain testimonials
 | 
Accredited training delivery
 | District officials, land professionals, NGOs, private companies, students
 | The eleven module curriculum in Annex 18
 | Package short courses; price them; seek recognition from a professional or academic body
 | 
Land due diligence and advisory for responsible investors
 | Investors, agribusiness, financial institutions
 | Legal and community expertise; VGGT knowledge
 | Draft a strict ethics screen so that no assignment can be used against communities; Board approval required for every client
 | 
Publications and tools
 | Practitioners, students, institutions
 | Manuals, guides and templates already produced
 | Produce priced editions while keeping community versions free
 | 
Facility and equipment hire
 | Local organisations and community groups
 | Training space and equipment
 | Set a rate card with free use for community structures
 | 
Two rules govern earned income. First, no assignment may be accepted that would harm the land rights of communities or compromise ARDHI independence, and the Board decides any borderline case. Second, community versions of any tool, guide or training remain free to communities regardless of what a paying client is charged.
