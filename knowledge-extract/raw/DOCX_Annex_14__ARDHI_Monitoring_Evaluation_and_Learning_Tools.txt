About ARDHI
We are a Ugandan based NGO that drives community resilience through advocacy and systems innovations in the areas of agriculture and food security, waste management, disaster preparedness, and land governance.
Vision
A future where research informs action, and action delivers justice.
Mission
Impacting communities through research, innovations and advocacy particularly in areas of smart agriculture, waste management, disaster preparedness, health and land governance.
Our Values
Sustainability: Champion policies and actions that safeguard natural resources.
Equity: Foster inclusive environments and advocate for fair access to resources, ensuring marginalized voices are represented and empowered.
Innovation: Pioneer creative solutions and leverage emerging technologies to address complex systemic challenges.
Collaboration: Forge purposeful partnerships to amplify collective impact and drive lasting change.
Our Theory of Change
ARDHI is not a Think Tank, but a Think and Act Tank. Established in 2024 and fully registered in 2025, we operate on the belief that if we combine advocacy to shift rules, research to generate empirical evidence, and innovation to pilot practical models across agriculture, waste, disaster preparedness, health, and land governance, communities can gain the tools, voice, and systems they need to solve persistent problems. Lasting impact requires more than just ideas, it requires pressure, proof, and prototypes working together.
Our offices are located at Plot 1207, Ggaba Road, Next to the American Embassy, Kampala, Uganda. For general enquiries, contact us at info@ardhi.org.ug or visit www.ardhi.org.ug.

1. Purpose of this Annex
This annex sets out how ARDHI Law and Policy Initiative plans, collects, checks, analyses, uses and shares information about its work. It contains both the framework that governs monitoring, evaluation and learning across the organisation, and the ready to use tools that project teams apply in the field.
ARDHI works on land and policy questions where change is slow, contested and hard to attribute to a single actor. A tick box approach to reporting would tell us nothing useful. Our system is therefore built to answer three questions honestly: are we doing what we said we would do, is it making a difference for the people whose land rights are insecure, and what should we do differently.
We measure in order to learn and to improve, not only in order to report.
1.1 Objectives of the MEL system
To track delivery against plans and budgets so that problems are seen early.
To measure results at output, outcome and impact level with credible evidence.
To provide disaggregated data that shows who benefited and who was left out.
To generate learning that changes practice within the life of a project, not only after it.
To meet donor reporting obligations accurately and on time.
To be accountable to the communities we serve by sharing results back with them.
1.2 Principles

Principle
 | What it means in practice
 | 
Utility
 | We collect only data that someone will use for a decision. Every indicator has a named user.
 | 
Credibility
 | Data is verifiable, traceable to a source document, and checked before it is reported.
 | 
Disaggregation
 | All people level data is disaggregated by sex, age band, disability status and, where relevant, tenure status and location.
 | 
Participation
 | Communities take part in setting indicators of change that matter to them, and in reviewing results.
 | 
Do no harm and confidentiality
 | Data collection never exposes a person to risk. Personal data is handled under the Data Protection and Privacy Act, 2019.
 | 
Honesty
 | Negative results, failed activities and missed targets are reported as fully as successes.
 | 
Timeliness
 | Data reaches the person who needs it while a decision can still be changed.
 | 
Proportionality
 | The size of the MEL effort matches the size and risk of the project.

4. Data Management and Quality
4.1 Data flow

Step
 | Description
 | 
1. Collection
 | Field Officers collect data using the standard tools in this annex, on paper or on mobile devices using KoboToolbox or ODK.
 | 
2. Submission
 | Data submitted to the MEL Officer within five working days of the activity.
 | 
3. Entry and cleaning
 | Data entered into the ARDHI MEL database, checked for duplicates, missing values and outliers.
 | 
4. Verification
 | Five percent of records verified against source documents, and a sample of participants contacted directly.
 | 
5. Analysis
 | Indicator values calculated, disaggregated, compared to target and to baseline.
 | 
6. Use
 | Results discussed at monthly and quarterly reviews and used to adjust plans.
 | 
7. Reporting
 | Results reported to donors, the Board and the communities.
 | 
8. Storage
 | Data archived securely, with personal identifiers separated from analysis files.
 | 4.2 Data quality standards

Dimension
 | Standard
 | How it is checked
 | 
Validity
 | The data measures what the indicator says it measures.
 | Indicator reference sheet reviewed at design and at each reporting period
 | 
Reliability
 | The same method produces the same result over time and across teams.
 | Standard tools, enumerator training, inter rater checks
 | 
Completeness
 | All expected records are present and all required fields are filled.
 | Submission tracker; no report accepted with missing fields
 | 
Timeliness
 | Data arrives in time to be used.
 | Five working day submission rule; monthly dashboard
 | 
Precision
 | Numbers are accurate and free of double counting.
 | Unique beneficiary identifiers; deduplication before reporting
 | 
Integrity
 | Data has not been manipulated.
 | Back checks on 5 percent of records; source documents retained; audit trail in the database
 | 4.3 Data protection
Informed consent is obtained before any personal data is collected, and the purpose is explained.
Unique beneficiary codes are used in analysis files, and names are held separately with restricted access.
Devices are password protected and encrypted, and data is uploaded and removed from devices within forty eight hours.
Access to personal data is limited by role and is logged.
Data on land disputes, on survivors of violence and on persons at risk is treated as sensitive and is never shared without written consent.
Personal data is retained only as long as the grant and the law require, and is then securely destroyed.

5. Learning
5.1 Learning agenda
Each project sets three to five learning questions at design. These are questions the team does not yet know the answer to and that would change how the work is done. Examples for ARDHI include:
Which combination of legal aid, community mediation and clan dialogue resolves land disputes most durably, and at what cost per case?
Does joint documentation of family land change how decisions are actually made in the household, or only what the paper says?
What makes a district land institution adopt a practice we promote, and what makes the change stick after our project ends?
Which channels reach young people and persons with disabilities with land rights information most effectively?
Under what conditions does strengthening the land rights of women create backlash, and what reduces it?

 | 5.3 From learning to action
Every learning event ends with an action list that names the change to be made, the person responsible and the date. The MEL Officer maintains the adaptation log and reports at the next quarterly review on whether each action was completed. Learning that is recorded but not acted on is treated as a system failure and is raised with the Country Director.

6. Evaluation

Evaluation type
 | When
 | Approach
 | 
Baseline study
 | Within 90 days of start
 | Household survey with a comparison group where feasible, key informant interviews, focus groups, review of land records
 | 
Midterm review
 | At the midpoint
 | Internal or light external review focused on progress, assumptions and adaptation
 | 
Final evaluation
 | Final quarter
 | Independent evaluation against the OECD DAC criteria of relevance, coherence, effectiveness, efficiency, impact and sustainability, with a gender and inclusion lens
 | 
Thematic or process evaluation
 | As needed
 | Deep study of one approach, for example community mediation, to inform scale up
 | 
Ex post follow up
 | 12 to 24 months after closure
 | Light study of whether results held, feeding the sustainability plan in Annex 20
 | 
Evaluation terms of reference always require: disaggregated analysis; direct consultation with women, youth and persons with disabilities; an assessment of unintended effects including harm; a clear statement of limitations; and a validation session with ARDHI and the communities before the report is finalised. Evaluators sign the ARDHI Code of Conduct and follow the ethics standards in Section 7.
7. Research and Data Ethics
Voluntary, informed consent from every respondent, with a clear right to decline or to stop at any time and no effect on their access to services.
Parental or guardian consent plus the assent of the child for any respondent under eighteen, and no interviews with children on sensitive matters without specialist arrangements.
No collection of data that ARDHI cannot protect or will not use.
Referral pathway available before any data collection that could surface distress, violence or a safeguarding disclosure.
No payment for participation, but reimbursement of reasonable transport costs.
Ethical clearance from a recognised research ethics committee for any study involving sensitive personal data or vulnerable groups.
Results returned to the communities that provided the data, in a form they can use.
