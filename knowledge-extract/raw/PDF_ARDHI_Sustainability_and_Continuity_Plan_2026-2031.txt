1. Plan Overview 
ARDHI operates as a Ugandan-based nonprofit that drives community resilience through advocacy and systems 
innovation in agriculture and food security, resilient waste management, disaster preparedness, public health, 
and inclusive land governance. This plan consolidates ARDHI's sustainability, continuity, and program logic into a 
single operating framework that reflects the organization's identity, priorities, and  public mandate. 
ARDHI's approach fits the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and Uganda's Fourth National Development 
Plan (NDP IV) because it links justice, livelihoods, public health, climate resilience, and institutional reform. The 
organization's core method is clear: research generates evidence, advocacy moves policy, and innovation 
converts ideas into practical community models.  
This document is the companion to ARDHI's 5-Year Strategic Plan (2026 –2031). Where the Strategic Plan sets out 
what ARDHI will do — its goals, programs, and yearly milestones. This Sustainability and Continuity Plan sets out 
how ARDHI will remain funded, functional, and durable while doing it, including under funding shocks, staff 
transitions, or external disruption.

2. Strategic Purpose 
ARDHI's purpose is to secure lasting community resilience through legal reform, practical innovation, and 
evidence-based advocacy. The organization exists to bridge lived realities and policy systems, especially where 
weak governance, food insecurity, waste crises, disaster risks, health inequalities, and land injustices affect 
vulnerable communities. 
Sustainability, therefore, is not separate from mission delivery; it is the mechanism that keeps mission delivery 
alive across time. A strategy without a sustainability plan is a set of intentions; a strategy with one is an operating 
institution.

4. The Sustainability Model — Five Pillars 
ARDHI sustains itself through five connected pillars: financial resilience, institutional durability, program 
continuity, partnership stability, and leadership depth. Each pillar is embedded in the organization's operating 
logic and treated as a standing responsibility rather than a future aspiration. The result is an organization capable 
of continuing impact even when funding, staffing, or external conditions change.

5. Financial Sustainability 
ARDHI uses a mixed-income model that includes grants, technical consulting, research services, training, 
convenings, and partnership-based implementation. Unrestricted income supports core operations, governance, 
continuity systems, and reserves, while restricted funding supports specific thematic delivery. No single revenue 
source is allowed to become structurally dominant. 
 
Figure 2: Target income diversification — no source exceeds 40% of total income 
5.1 Reserve Policy 
A reserve policy equivalent to at least three months of essential operating costs protects the organization against 
delayed disbursements, donor transitions, or emergency interruptions. This reserve is built deliberately into the 
annual budget (see the ARDHI 5-Year Strategic Plan, Section 10.2) rather than left to year -end surplus, growing 
from half a month of cover in Year 1 to a full four months of cover by Year 5.

5.3 Financial Discipline 
Cash-flow forecasting, income tracking, and grant -pipeline management form part of routine financial discipline, 
reviewed monthly by the Finance & Policy directorate and quarterly by the Board's Finance & Audit Committee.  
• Cost-recovery pricing — Consultancy and training rates are set to recover full cost plus a modest 
contribution to unrestricted reserves. 
• Multi-year grant preference — Priority given to donors offering 2–3 year commitments over single-year 
project grants, to reduce annual fundraising pressure.  
• Currency risk management — Multi-currency grant agreements sought where possible; quarterly UGX 
budget reforecasting against inflation and exchange-rate movement.

6. Institutional Sustainability  
ARDHI's institutional memory lives in manuals, templates, policy briefs, research archives, toolkits, and 
standardized operating procedures. Every major activity produces a reusable output that can be applied across 
districts, partnerships, or future projects. This keeps the organization's knowledge inside the institution rather 
than in individual staff members. 
Governance rests on a clear delegation structure, board oversight, and documented approval pathways. Critical 
responsibilities have deputies and handover notes so that continuity does not collapse when personnel change. 
Annual institutional reviews assess whether systems remain fit for growth, compliance, and resilience.  
6.1 Knowledge Management System 
• Central repository — All manuals, templates, research outputs, and SOPs are stored in a single, access -
controlled knowledge base maintained by the Research & Resource directorate. 
• Reusable-output standard — Every project, pilot, or engagement is required to produce at least one 
reusable artefact (template, brief, toolkit, or dataset) before closeout.  
• Version control — Policy positions, brand assets, and core templates are version-controlled, with a named 
owner for each document class. 
6.2 Governance Pathways 
The Board provides strategic oversight through three committees (Finance & Audit; HR & Governance; 
Programmes & Strategy), while the Executive Director and four operating directorates handle execution — as set 
out in the ARDHI 5-Year Strategic Plan, Section 8. Every major decision has a documented approval pathway, and 
an annual institutional review assesses whether governance, financial, and knowledge system s remain fit for the 
organization's stage of growth.

7. Advocacy, Research and Innovation Logic  
ARDHI's advocacy translates community concerns into policy influence, legal reform, and public accountability. Its 
research documents land injustices, policy gaps, crisis patterns, and practical lessons from the field. Its 
innovations convert those lessons into tools, pilots, models, and community -ready solutions. 
 
Figure 5: The continuous Advocacy–Research–Innovation learning cycle 
These three functions operate as one cycle. Research produces evidence, advocacy converts evidence into 
influence, and innovation tests practical responses that communities and institutions can use. That cycle defines 
ARDHI's identity and makes the organization durable because it cr eates both social legitimacy and operational 
relevance.

8. Thematic Delivery and Continuity  
Each of ARDHI's six thematic pillars applies the same Advocacy –Research–Innovation cycle, but plays a distinct 
role in the organization's long-term durability. Together, they make ARDHI's mission outlast any single project, 
donor, or staff cohort. 
ArdhiLaw 
Access to justice, land rights, fair policy, and accountability. Research documents legal gaps and rights 
violations; advocacy pushes reform; innovation produces model contracts, legal literacy materials, and 
dispute-resolution tools. Law is a continuity pillar because legal literacy and policy influence outlast 
individual projects. 
ArdhiAgriculture 
Climate-smart agriculture, food system resilience, and nutrition-sensitive community practice. 
Research identifies production and access constraints; advocacy calls for supportive policy; innovation 
pilots better farming practices and local food-security solutions. Aligns directly with SDG 2 and NDP IV's 
agricultural transformation agenda. 
ArdhiDisasters 
Early-warning thinking, inclusive evacuation planning, and community-first disaster response. Research 
maps risk and institutional gaps; advocacy pressures authorities to plan and act earlier; innovation 
creates preparedness tools, drills, and coordination models. Becomes sustainable when communities 
and institutions adopt these systems as routine practice. 
ArdhiWaste 
Safer systems, recycling, circular-economy thinking, and environmental-health advocacy. Research 
tracks the social and health cost of waste crises; advocacy pushes for safer municipal policy; innovation 
pilots segregation, recovery, and youth-engagement pathways. Directly supports SDG 12 and reinforces 
public-health protection. 
ArdhiHealth 
Links environmental health to human health — clean air, safe water, maternal health, and community 
awareness. Research shows how pollution, waste and insecure settlements affect health; advocacy 
pushes for healthier policy environments; innovation supports practical prevention interventions. A 
cross-cutting sustainability pillar rather than a stand-alone sector. 
ArdhiLandGovernance 
Fair land policies, community rights, legal literacy, and equitable land ownership. Research documents 
injustices and policy failures; advocacy convenes reform champions; innovation produces model 
contracts and community training materials. Connects directly to SDG 16 and the justice dimension of 
sustainable development.

9. Partnership System 
ARDHI treats partnerships as implementation infrastructure. Local governments, universities, civil society, faith -
based institutions, communities, and private actors serve as co-producers of change rather than passive 
stakeholders. Formal agreements define shared objectives, roles, re porting expectations, and transition 
arrangements. 
Partnerships strengthen reach, credibility, and uptake. Joint planning, co -funding, shared data, and collective 
learning keep relationships useful over time. This makes ARDHI's work more durable because it  is anchored in 
networks rather than in isolation. 
9.1 Partnership Standards 
• Formal agreements — Every significant partnership is governed by a written MOU defining shared 
objectives, roles, reporting expectations, and transition/exit arrangements.  
• Joint planning & co-funding — Partners contribute funding, in-kind resources, technical expertise, or 
convening power alongside ARDHI, reducing per-partner delivery cost. 
• Shared data & collective learning — Partners contribute to and draw from ARDHI's knowledge repository 
(Section 6.1), keeping relationships useful and current over time.  
• Annual partner review — Each active MOU is reviewed annually against agreed objectives, informing 
renewal, adjustment, or graceful exit.

10. People and Leadership 
ARDHI invests in staff learning, mentoring, role redundancy, and leadership preparation. Every critical role has a 
back-up, and every major function has documented procedures for continuity. The organization treats learning as 
part of performance, so knowledge is constantly transferred and refreshed.  
Leadership continuity depends on a bench of prepared people rather than on one central figure. Succession 
planning, delegation, coaching, and handover systems protect the organization from disruption and preserve 
institutional memory. 
10.1 Succession Practices 
• Named deputies — Every directorate lead and the Executive Director have a named deputy briefed on core 
responsibilities. 
• Handover documentation — Role handover notes are maintained continuously, not written only when 
someone resigns. 
• Coaching & mentoring — Senior staff mentor at least one junior or associate colleague toward readiness for 
greater responsibility. 
• Board succession — Board terms and re-election cycles are staggered to avoid simultaneous turnover of 
governance oversight.

11.1 Crisis Protocols 
Crisis protocols cover staff safety, communications, service continuity, and emergency response. Remote access, 
backups, incident reporting, and emergency contacts support operational resilience — making the organization 
capable of functioning during disruption rather than only in stable conditions.  
• Staff safety & communications — Documented protocols for field-work safety, crisis communications to 
staff and stakeholders, and an emergency contact tree.  
• Service continuity — Remote-access arrangements and cross-trained deputies (Section 10.1) allow core 
functions to continue during disruption. 
• Data backups & incident reporting — Regular backups of research, financial, and program data, with a 
defined incident-reporting line for safeguarding, security, or data -protection concerns. 
• Program suspension protocol — A documented, ethical process for pausing or winding down a program 
safely if funding or context requires it, protecting beneficiaries and partners.

14. Exit and Transition Strategy 
A sustainability plan is incomplete without a plan for how ARDHI's models, programs, and partnerships eventually 
stand on their own. ARDHI treats graduation and transition as a deliberate design feature, not an afterthought 
triggered by a funding gap. 
14.1 Program Graduation Criteria 
A pilot or program is considered ready for institutionalization or handover when it meets the following criteria, 
assessed at each annual review: 
• Evidence of adoption — A partner institution, local government, or community structure has adopted or co-
funded the model without requiring ARDHI's continued direct delivery.  
• Documented replication package — A manual, toolkit, or training package exists that allows the model to be 
delivered by others. 
• Local capacity in place — Community-based structures, local government units, or partner staff have the 
skills to sustain the model. 
• Financial independence — The model no longer depends on ARDHI's core budget, having secured its own 
funding line or been absorbed into a partner's recurrent budget. 
14.2 Transition Pathways 
• Government handover — Models with strong public-good characteristics (e.g., disaster-preparedness 
protocols, land-dispute tools) are transitioned to local government or relevant ministries for institutional 
adoption. 
• Community ownership — Community-based models (e.g., waste segregation groups, farmer learning circles) 
are transitioned to community structures with a light -touch ARDHI advisory role. 
• Partner-led scale-up — Proven models are licensed or co-branded for delivery by partner NGOs or private 
actors with wider reach or resources. 
• Responsible sunset — Where a model does not achieve traction, it is retired transparently, with lessons 
captured in the knowledge repository (Section 6.1) rather than quietly abandoned.  
Every transition is documented in a short Transition Note: what is being handed over, to whom, under what 
support arrangement, and how success will be tracked after ARDHI's direct role ends.
